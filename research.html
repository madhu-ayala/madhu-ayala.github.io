Job Market Paper

The Conditional Carbon Premium: Climate Policy Path and Expected Returns

Abstract

How does the climate policy path affect the carbon risk premium? I develop a theoretical model of a stochastic production economy to compare early and uncertain delayed policy implementation to achieve the same temperature target. When policy is implemented early, the carbon tax that implements the target rises in high-productivity states, when the stochastic discount factor is low, making carbon-intensive firms a hedge with lower expected returns. When policy is delayed, each year of waiting raises the adjustment required on arrival. A late arrival reduces the value of carbon-intensive firms while raising the stochastic discount factor, generating a positive risk premium. Empirically, I estimate a Markov-switching model on the implied cost of capital of portfolios of brown and green firms. While the brown-minus-green implied cost of capital shows a full 2013–2024 sample average close to zero, the sample is also associated with persistent periods of positive and negative premia. On the one hand, periods characterized by a negative carbon premium tend to be associated with climate-friendly events, suggesting an orderly transition. On the other hand, periods characterized by a positive carbon premium tend to be associated with setbacks, suggesting at best a disorderly transition. The magnitude of the positive premium also suggests a late arrival of the switch to a net-zero transition.

The Dual Strategy of Exclusion and Engagement: Impact on Asset Prices and Green Transition

with Eric Jondeau

Abstract

This paper develops a theoretical asset-pricing model to examine how sustainable investors can combine exclusion and engagement strategies to accelerate corporate transition. Firms are classified as green, brown, or reformable, with the latter being polluting firms that can reduce emissions under shareholder pressure. Sustainable investors exclude brown firms but may engage with reformable ones when majority ownership enables them to enforce a transition. Engagement is modeled as a costly but effective mechanism that lowers emissions and generates non-pecuniary benefits for investors. Our main result is that only a moderate share of sustainable investors (around 22.5% of market wealth) is sufficient to trigger reformable firms’ transition, provided they derive a modest non-pecuniary benefit (about 2.3%) from sustainability improvements. In this equilibrium, sustainable investors are willing to concentrate their portfolios in reformable assets, enabling these firms to adopt cleaner technologies and reduce their environmental footprint. The model shows that a relatively small but motivated coalition of investors can induce meaningful environmental change through targeted engagement.

Active ETF Entry and the Reallocation of Active Capital

with Ziwei Zhao

Abstract

Active ETFs have become an increasingly important part of delegated asset management, raising the question of how their growth affects incumbent active mutual funds. We show that greater active ETF presence is associated with lower incumbent active mutual fund flows, while short-run exit effects remain limited. Using category-level variation in the entry of major active ETF families, we find that displacement is strongest in more fragmented categories and among smaller and younger funds. We also show that greater active ETF penetration is associated with higher concentration among surviving active mutual funds. We interpret these patterns through a simple framework in which ETF entry reallocates the more mobile portion of category assets. Our evidence suggests that active ETF growth reshapes active mutual fund markets through reallocation rather than immediate fund disappearance.
