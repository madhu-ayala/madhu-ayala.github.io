The Conditional Carbon Premium: Climate Policy Path and Expected Returns

Abstract

How does the climate policy path affect the carbon risk premium? I develop a theoretical model of a stochastic production economy to compare early and uncertain delayed policy implementation to achieve the same temperature target. In a scenario where policy is implemented early, the carbon tax that implements the target rises in high productivity states, making carbon-intensive firms a hedge with lower expected returns. When policy is delayed, each year of waiting raises the adjustment required on arrival, and a late arrival imposes losses on carbon-intensive firms in bad times, turning the premium positive for later arrivals. Empirically, the brown-minus-green implied cost of capital shows persistent periods of premia of both signs. An average carbon premium therefore pools required-return differences of opposing signs.

The Dual Strategy of Exclusion and Engagement: Impact on Asset Prices and Green Transition

with Eric Jondeau

Abstract

This paper develops a theoretical asset pricing model to examine how investors combine exclusion and engagement strategies to have an impact on corporate transition. In our equilibrium model, we distinguish between green, brown, and reformable firms, the latter being polluting firms with potential to transition under shareholder pressure. Sustainable investors exclude brown firms but may engage with reformable ones if they expect a successful transition and derive sufficient non-pecuniary utility. Engagement is modeled as a costly effort yielding social benefit only if investors reach majority ownership. Our key finding is that a moderate share of sustainable investors, 22.5% of market wealth, is sufficient to trigger firm-level transition, provided their non-pecuniary utility exceeds the cost of engagement by a moderate 2.3%. In this equilibrium, reformable firms transition under sustainable investors pressure. The model shows how a relatively small but motivated group of green activists can drive meaningful environmental change through targeted engagement, reshaping both corporate behavior and asset pricing.

Active ETF Entry and the Reallocation of Active Capital

with Ziwei Zhao

Abstract

Active ETFs have become an increasingly important part of delegated asset management, raising the question of how their growth affects incumbent active mutual funds. We show that greater active ETF presence is associated with lower incumbent active mutual fund flows, while short-run exit effects remain limited. Using category-level variation in the entry of major active ETF families, we find that displacement is strongest in more fragmented categories and among smaller and younger funds. We also show that greater active ETF penetration is associated with higher concentration among surviving active mutual funds. We interpret these patterns through a simple framework in which ETF entry reallocates the more mobile portion of category assets. Our evidence suggests that active ETF growth reshapes active mutual fund markets through reallocation rather than immediate fund disappearance.
